--- title: "Midterm Elections 2014: Whither Immigration Reform?" date: 2014-11-05T05:30:00 url: "/post/midterm-elections-2014-whither-immigration-reform/" aliases: ["/immigration/2014/11/midterm-elections-2014-whither-immigration-reform.html"] type: post year: "2014" category: "Current Affairs" category_slug: "current-affairs" byline: "Immigration Prof" ---

With the Republican Party taking control of the Senate (and the distinct possibility that Mitch McConnell will be Senate Majority Leader)  in what CNN characterizes as "A Thumping GOP Win," and the GOP continuing to control the House of Representatives, the future of immigration reform is far from certain. 

On the one hand, it seems highly unlikely that this Republican Congress will pass anything like comprehensive immigration reform akin to Senate Bill 744 in the near future. 

On the other hand, the White House for its part does not appear to be backing down from the threat of taking executive action of some kind on immigration in the last two years of the Obama Presidency.  Often pointing to the congressional failure to act on immigration, President Obama could well surprise the nation -- as he did with the Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals (DACA) program in 2012, which has provided temporary relief to hundreds of thousands of noncitizens brought to the United States as children.  Given that he has consistently promised to do something on immigration, and has tantalized many immigrant rights advocates with promises to act in recent months, it would be difficult for him to back down from taking decisive immigration action.       

When it comes to immigration, the Obama administration has performed at its best when it has been decisive.  DACA was one example, finely conceptualized and effectively, efficiently, and quickly implemented.  Similarly, Department of Homeland Security Janet Napolitano soon after the Supreme Court decision invalidating the Defense of Marriage Act directed the immigration bureaucracy to process visa applications from same-sex partners. At the same time, the administration's perceived indecisiveness on moving forward with immigration reform has frustrated Latinos and immigrant right activists (as evidenced by the heckling of the President at a campaign rally earlier this week).

The Tuesday elections make a few things clear.   The Obama administration is in its final two years, with the President a true lame duck and low approval ratings. Republicans control Congress.  The President, who was mostly in the shadows as Democrats campaigned this fall, has been weakened.  The question remains whether the President will attempt to deliver on the promises he made on immigration reform when he successfully ran for President in 2008, inspired the nation, and built a multiracial coalition in the process.
