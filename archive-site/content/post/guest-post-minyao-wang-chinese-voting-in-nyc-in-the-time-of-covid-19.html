Guest Post -- Minyao Wang: Chinese Voting in NYC in the Time of COVID-19

Early voting in New York has begun.  This is the first presidential election during which the people of New York can cast ballots early.  Because of the COVID-19 pandemic, many voters decided to take advantage of this new option.  After only two days, almost 200,000 people in New York City have voted.  Naturalized Chinese-born citizens are no exception.  Early poll sites in Chinese enclaves are reporting very long lines.  As a bilingual attorney, I spent the past weekend doing “election protection” work.  More specifically, I helped monitor the City’s compliance with the federal statutory requirements to accommodate voters who are not proficient in English.

Two provisions of the landmark Voting Rights Act of 1965, as amended, are at play here.  Section 208 of the statute states that a voter located anywhere in the country “who requires assistance to vote by reason of blindness, disability, or inability to read or write” can be assisted by any person of her choice, other than her employer or a person associated with her union.  This provision has been interpreted to permit a naturalized citizen not proficient in English to bring anyone she is comfortable with (usually a family member or a trusted friend) to the voting booth to assist her, a pretty common practice nationwide.  The courts have held that Section 208 preempts state and local laws that impose limitations on voter assistance.  See OCA-Greater Houston v. Texas, 867 F. 3d 604, 614-15 (5th Cir. 2017) (interpreting “to vote” broadly and invalidating a state provision requiring that an interpreter be registered to vote in the voter's county of residence).

Section 203 of the Act requires “covered” jurisdictions to translate ballots, voter registration forms, voting instructions, and all other voting materials and provide in-person interpreters at election precincts.  For Section 203 to apply to a jurisdiction, the Census Bureau must find 5% or more than 10,000 voting-age citizens speak a foreign language at home, have limited English proficiency, and have a higher illiteracy rate than the national illiteracy rate.  The list is updated every five years based on Census data.  Currently, three boroughs of New York City (Manhattan, Queens and Brooklyn) meet the metrics for Chinese language.  Across the country, the following jurisdictions are currently required by Section 203 to provide Chinese language election assistance:

  • Honolulu
  • Chicago
  • Houston
  • Seattle
  • Malden and Quincy City (appropriately, the birthplace for Presidents John Adams and John Quincy Adams) in Massachusetts
  • Nine counties in California (Alameda, Contra Costa, Los Angeles, Orange, Sacramento, San Diego, San Francisco, Santa Clara, and San Mateo)

Compliance with Section 203 can be expensive and burdensome on local governments.  As of 2006, Los Angeles County spent $3 million a year providing multilingual voting services.  And New York City’s Board of Elections initially pleaded impossibility of performance, stating that Chinese characters could not fit into the voting machines then in use.  They relented only after a civil rights group, with the help of a printer and a graphic designer, created a bilingual sample ballot.  

Voting is the life blood of our democracy.  Chief Justice Earl Warren once wrote that the “right to vote in a free and unimpaired manner is preservative of other basic human and political rights.”  Reynolds v. Sims, 377 U.S. 533, 561-62 (1964).  Voting is how the American people have meaningful control over their government, and ultimately, their national destiny.  The unusual lengths to which the United States goes to ensure that newly naturalized citizens with limited English proficiency can exercise this fundamental right to vote reflect both how seriously we take our democracy and our self-confidence as a society.  We seek to include as many voices in political discussion as possible and to incorporate pluralistic concerns into our policy-making process.  Even for insular groups lacking the numerical strength to affect the outcome of elections, we seek and value their input because it is conducive to building legitimacy for and acceptance of the outcomes of our political process. 

Meanwhile, the high turnout among New York City’s first-generation Chinese immigrants bodes well for their integration into American society.  As I watched them struggle to fill the ovals on their ballots, it occurred to me that this is likely their first time ever seeing a ballot of any kind.  They after all left a country ruled by a brutal communist dictatorship that deprives ordinary people of their most basic human dignity.  They chose to live in America for its longstanding commitment to freedom and democracy.  I reminded myself of Census data showing that in the first decade of this century, 72,000 Chinese nationals settled in New York City under a grant of political asylum (the corresponding number nationwide is 140,000).  And the statistics for the second decade (which have not been officially released) are likely to be even higher, judging by the number of Chinese asylum applications filed and the rapidly worsening conditions in China.  Indeed, due to the upsurge of Chinese asylum applicants, the Chinese are projected to soon become the largest foreign-born group in New York City, which will be a demographic milestone in this most diverse metropolitan center on earth.   

KJ
