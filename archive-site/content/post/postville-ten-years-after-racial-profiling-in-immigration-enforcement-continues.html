In the aftermath of a massive immigration raid rural Iowa ten years ago  that grabbed grabbed headlines across the nation, see Interpreting after the Largest ICE Raid in U.S. History,   Erik Camayd-Freixas, an interpreter for the U.S. government, helped to bring light to the violation of the rights of immigrants that he witnessed.  Here’s his op-ed on the 10th anniversary of the Postville nightmare:

 
“This month marks the 10th anniversary of the Postville raid: More than 300 meatpackers were arrested on anti-terrorist charges of aggravated identity theft.

Most were illiterate Mayans from Guatemala who did not even know what a Social Security number was. At the plant, they helped them with their papers and the $550 fee, payroll-deductible.

As an interpreter in the prosecutions, I learned that the feds coerced them into waiving their constitutional right to grand jury indictment, under threat of more jail time. Then they were denied a bail hearing, because ICE’s immigration detainer compelled the magistrates. An onerous plea agreement coerced them into pleading guilty to Social Security fraud with six months in prison, under threat of 2-10 years for aggravated identity theft. 

They were begging to be deported, but we first had to put them in chains. 

At stake was the survival of their children, who depended on their measly remittances.

“A day I don’t work is a day my children don’t eat,” one man told me.

I witnessed how that plea agreement bound the judges so they had no discretion at sentencing. Executive agencies Immigration and Customs Enforcement, Department of Human Services and Department of Justice forced the judiciary. It was the breakdown of the separation of powers, a pillar of our democracy. It was also the only mass felony prosecution in American legal history: Almost 400 souls processed, convicted and sentenced, 10 at a time, in just seven working days.

They were denied the universal right of defense. I denounced the abuse in an essay that helped secure a unanimous U.S. Supreme Court ruling (USSC 08-108) to disallow identity theft charges against unknowing migrants, removing the threat of frivolous prosecution from 7 million undocumented workers.

Still, Postville and the neighboring economy were devastated. A huge lesson: Immigrant jobs create higher-level opportunities for Americans. And a repeating pattern: American companies bankrupted by the raids, sold for scraps to foreign interests, which now control most of our meat industry. The saddest was the demise of the 150-year-old Swift & Co. Thousands of higher-level jobs were lost for Americans. 

The Postville operation was a pilot to be scaled across the country. It involved massive interagency coordination: 1,100 agents, 700 arrest warrants, two Blackhawk helicopters, 10 DHS prison-transfer buses, 23 FEMA trailers, nine judges, 34 prosecutors, 23 court-appointed defenders, 26 interpreters, a multimillion-dollar venture.

Our last immigration reform, IIRIRA 1996, established mandatory and indefinite detention, without charges, but we lacked today’s integrated databases and surveillance capabilities. Yet in this brave new world of Homeland Security, immigration enforcement has become the systematic persecution of a profiled population, the stuff of totalitarian regimes. George Orwell was mistaken: 1984 is now.”

Erik Camayd-Freixas is professor of Hispanic studies at Florida International University. camayde@fiu.edu
