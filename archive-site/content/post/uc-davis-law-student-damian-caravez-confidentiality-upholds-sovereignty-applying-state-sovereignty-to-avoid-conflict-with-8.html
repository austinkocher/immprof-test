--- title: "UC Davis Law Student Damian Caravez: Confidentiality Upholds Sovereignty: Applying State Sovereignty to Avoid Conflict with 8 U.S.C. § 1373 and Protect Sanctuary Policies" date: 2017-05-01T13:53:57 url: "/post/uc-davis-law-student-damian-caravez-confidentiality-upholds-sovereignty-applying-state-sovereignty-to-avoid-conflict-with-8/" aliases: ["/immigration/2017/05/uc-davis-law-student-damian-caravez-confidentiality-upholds-sovereignty-applying-state-sovereignty-to-avoid-conflict-with-8.html"] type: post year: "2017" category: "Current Affairs" category_slug: "current-affairs" byline: "Immigration Prof" ---

A few days ago, the U.S. Department of Justice (“DOJ”) issued a letter to law enforcement agencies in California, Chicago, New York City, Philadelphia, Las Vegas, New Orleans, Miami, and Milwaukee notifying them to prove compliance with 8 U.S.C. § 1373 or else risk suspension of future Byrne Justice Assistance Grant (“JAG”) awards. The grants provide state and local jurisdictions funds to support a variety of law enforcement programs.  8 U.S.C. § 1373 is the federal statute prohibiting government entities from restricting the exchange of an individual’s immigration status between any other government official or entity, including United States Citizenship and Immigration Services (“USCIS”) and Immigration and Customs Enforcement (“ICE”). By sending the letter to the law enforcement agencies, the DOJ has essentially informed them that they are “sanctuary cities”—cities that have policies to protect immigrants from Federal immigration officials— because they prohibit local officials from sharing information about a person’s immigration status with Federal immigration agencies under local laws. The JAG awards are essential funds for crime prevention and law enforcement in many local jurisdictions. In 2015 alone, the DOJ allocated $255.8 million to JAG grants.    Withholding JAG awards may cripple local law enforcement functions and local jurisdictions should turn to the law to defend their protective policies.

While there are many ways to challenge the executive order and DOJ letters, the Second Circuit’s reasoning in City of New York v. United States provides one method to strengthen and validate local policies that protect an individual’s immigration status. A robust confidentiality policy combined with clearly delineated disclosure requirements can be one way sanctuary cities avoid conflict with Federal statutory law to avoid a potential loss of Federal funds.

 In New York, the Second Circuit held constitutional 8 U.S.C. § 1373. The court ruled that the statute did not intrude on state sovereignty principles under Gregory v. Ashcroft and the Tenth Amendment that give local entities the right to define the duties of their employees. The Second Circuit believed that § 1373 “did not impermissibly intrude on the city’s power to control information obtained in the course of official business or to regulate the duties and responsibilities of [its] employees” because New York City’s policy prohibiting its employees from sharing immigrants’ status with Federal officials was a narrow rule that did not truly protect immigrants’ confidentiality—the city could share this information with any other agency. This had the effect of leaving New York City in violation of § 1373.

However, in New York, the Second Circuit provided sanctuary cities (and New York City) a useful legal basis to implement stronger confidentiality policies that can potentially protect immigrant status from Federal immigration officials. The Court held that the obtaining of pertinent information is essential to a city’s local functions and that confidentiality is often necessary to effectuate these functions. In turn, a functional city requires that a city be able to regulate employee use of confidential information. So, if immigrant status was a part of local general confidentiality laws, used to carry out local functions, one’s status was truly confidential. Thus, a city policy directing its employees to maintain immigrant status confidential as part of a greater confidentiality scheme is likely within the city’s permissible authority as an agent of the state.

Section 1373 would then insult Tenth Amendment constitutional principles, and § 9(a) of the President’s executive order would offend state sovereignty.

 In New York, the Second Circuit asked the city for examples of confidentiality policies that were integral to the city’s functions. Apart from encouraging people report local crime, New York offered no tangible examples to show that immigrant status confidentiality was essential to the its function.

Fortunately, privacy rules at the University of California can provide a helpful answer. The University has a general confidentiality policy to prevent disclosure of personal student (including noncitizen student) information. See Policies Applying to Campus Activities, Organizations and Students (PACAOS). While immigration status is not currently classified confidential at the University, the University can classify this information confidential upon review. Doing so will place immigrant status in a generalized confidentiality category that can be accessed only by University officials. This is the type of rule the Second Circuit suggests local entities can direct their employees to protect. The essential local function the University’s confidentiality policy achieves, what the Second Circuit suggests is must be shown to validate the policy, is to distribute student benefits, promote a safe learning environment, and mitigate stigmas associated with immigrant status.

Moreover, under the Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act and USA PATRIOT Act, if the University chooses to classify immigrant status as confidential, such an act would require Federal officials to obtain a subpoena for a person’s immigrant status. The requirements of those acts avoid the ease of disclosure between local and federal agencies mandated by § 1373.

 One may counter and argue that categorizing immigrant status into general confidentiality rules does not truly help a city conduct essential local functions. This point can easily be surmounted. The University of California’s policy is an example of one legitimate confidentiality scheme. The New York court offered another: to encourage city residents to report crime without fear that their immigrant status will be sought or used against them. San Francisco protects immigrant status to offer medical care to all residents regardless of status and secure their general welfare. In sum, there are multiple legitimate reasons local governments want to maintain immigrant status confidential to ensure the proper operation of their essential functions.

In addition, a city that has specific procedures to allow local agencies and employees to disclose immigrant status does not exclude the Federal government from this information completely. Like San Francisco, cities may authorize disclosure in situations where the individual has committed violent felonies. If President Trump’s executive order prioritizes the detention and removal of those who have committed dangerous acts, specific disclosure requirements do not conflict because they too prioritize individuals that commit similar acts.

In sum, a confidentiality policy does not conflict with the Second Circuit’s holding that § 1373 mandates voluntary and unrestricted cooperation between local and federal officials if a local entity includes immigrant status in a thorough confidentiality policy. State sovereignty principles take precedence over § 1373 because the duties of local officials require compliance with confidentiality policies to ensure the efficient function of local government. In this way, the Second Circuit’s concerns are diminished and sovereignty principles can provide a line of defense against possible Federal sanction.
