Antitrust from Immigrants and Undocumented People by Gregory Day, Cornell Law Review, Forthcoming

Abstract

Immigrants and undocumented people encounter discrimination because they compete against “native” businesses and workers. When protectionism leads to discrimination, bigoted rhetoric has played a critical role, resulting in protests, boycotts, and even violence meant to exclude immigrants from markets. Key to this story is government’s ability to discriminate as well: it is indeed common for state and federal actors to protect citizens by enacting laws and regulations meant to prevent immigrants from braiding hair, manicuring nails, operating food trucks, or otherwise competing. But antitrust courts have hardly ever mentioned a person’s immigration status, much less offered a remedy.

This Article shows that antitrust’s “consumer welfare” standard was almost surgically designed to ignore immigrants and other marginalized people. It is often recited that antitrust law is a “color blind” regime designed to benefit consumers collectively, meaning that it is not supposed to prioritize insular groups such as immigrants. Courts and scholars have also described matters of inequality and discrimination as “social harms” existing beyond antitrust’s scope. Furthermore, antitrust lawsuits have successfully sought to drive immigrants out of markets, alleging that competitors gained an “unfair” advantage from employing undocumented workers. Under this view of antitrust law, the exclusion of immigrants is an appropriate way of promoting competition.

This Article argues that anti-immigrant discrimination creates the exact types of harms that antitrust was meant to remedy. Since excluding immigrants misallocates resources on citizenship or racial lines as opposed to their most productive usages, certain acts of discrimination should entail “conduct without a legitimate business purpose” even when based solely on racial animus. A hidden type of market power is revealed in that foreign-born people are less able to employ self-help remedies to correct market failures. In addition to analyzing antitrust’s purpose and economic foundation, the Article delves into antitrust’s history — a key source of authority — to show that an original goal of competition law was to protect foreigners. By showing how incumbents can inflict greater levels of harm on immigrants while wielding less market power, this Article exposes flaws in the consumer welfare standard and its colorblind approach, reveals how marginalized communities defy antitrust’s assumptions of self-help, and promotes the rights of immigrants.
