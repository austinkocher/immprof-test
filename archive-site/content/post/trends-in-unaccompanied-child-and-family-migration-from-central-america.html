The child migration crisis dominated the headlines in spring and summer 2014, as thousands of unaccompanied children from El Salvador, Guatemala, and Honduras arrived at the U.S.-Mexico border. The issue receded from significant public attention as the numbers dropped after interventions by governments in the United States, Mexico, and Central America.

However, the number of arrivals of unaccompanied children and families from Central America’s Northern Triangle began to tick up again in the latter half of 2015. A new Migration Policy Institute fact sheet offers a timely reminder that significant humanitarian and migration pressures in the troubled region continue to exist.

In Trends in Unaccompanied Child and Family Migration from Central America, Marc Rosenblum and Isabel Ball trace the evolving trends in child and family migration, using the most recent apprehensions data from the United States and Mexico. The fact sheet examines the factors that contributed to the downturn in migration. They include increased enforcement by the United States and, in particular, Mexico, with the advent of its Southern Border Program. Guatemala and Honduras also took additional steps to secure their common borders and Guatemala’s border with Mexico; and the three Northern Triangle countries announced a large-scale development strategy known as the Plan for the Alliance for Prosperity. The U.S. Congress recently agreed to provide $750 million to support the Plan.


The fact sheet also sketches the push factors that continue to drive this migration—violence, endemic poverty, food insecurity, political instability, and corruption—and the pull factors, which include years-long backlogs in U.S. immigration courts and large numbers of Central Americans already living in the United States. Thus, U.S. relatives and communities are a logical destination for those fleeing violence or otherwise seeking to improve their lives.

As the authors conclude, “Until these underlying conditions change, the flows can be expected to persist.”
