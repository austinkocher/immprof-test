Investigations by the NY Times and Reuters reveal that the US has been aware of child labor in immigrant families, but it has rarely acted on it. Hannah Dreier for the NY Times interviewed 100 child laborers in 20 states who work in many sectors. (The Biden administration found 835 companies hired 3,800 children in violation of labor laws). These include major factories that produce Cheerios, sew for J. Crew or Fruit of the Loom, and make auto parts used by Ford and General Motors. In the service sector, the children bake dinner rolls sold at Walmart and Target, process milk used in Ben & Jerry’s ice cream, debone chicken sold at Whole Foods, harvest coffee, deliver meals, and wash hotel sheets in Virginia. Children can even pick tobacco legally due to labor exceptions for agriculture, according to NPR and the Atlantic.

Many of the migrant children are unaccompanied minors living with family sponsors identified by the U.S. government. They face pressure from the foster families to earn money and their biological families to send remittances or repay debt for their migration journey. As minors, they have to juggle school with their workloads. (The NY Times profiles contain vivid stories.)

How did this happen? Hiring underage children for this type of work is against the law, but the practice has proliferated -- an increase of 69%, according to the Labor Department -- as protections for migrant children have weakened in the years since the pandemic. The Department of Labor's wage and hour and workplace safety investigators are chronically understaffed, the Health and Human Services investigators  responsible for identifying foster families have become lax, and state agencies are similarly underresourced, explains a former Department of Labor official on NPR. An article in Slate adds that "fissured" workplaces rely on staffing agencies that do not always check paperwork. 

The Biden administration's Labor Department and HHS are launching a joint task force to address the problem of child labor exploitation.
