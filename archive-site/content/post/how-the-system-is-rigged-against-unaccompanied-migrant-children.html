In a powerful new piece for Rewire, Tina Vasquez explores the plight of undocumented teens who, fleeing violence in their countries of origin, presented themselves at the border in the hopes of finding safety and asylum in the United States. But due to an immigration system that is seemingly rigged against them, this vulnerable population is being stripped of basic human rights and protections.

Since launching in January of this year, Operation Border Guardian has disrupted the lives of hundreds of Central American migrants who came to the U.S. as unaccompanied minors. Many were detained and, according to advocates, are still in the custody of Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE), deprived of an education and awaiting deportation.

Despite statements from the Obama administration that ICE would focus its resources on the “highest priorities,” individuals who might pose a threat to public safety, rather than asylum-seeking high school students, ICE’s own words and actions reflect a different reality.

As Vasquez writes:

ICE picked up some 121 people in the early January raids, primarily women and their young children. Advocates argue many of those arrested were detained unlawfully, because as people who experienced severe trauma and exhibited symptoms of post-traumatic stress disorder, generalized anxiety and depression, they met the definition of “disabled” under the Rehabilitation Act of 1973, and ICE did not provide reasonable accommodations to ensure disabled people were not denied meaningful access to benefits or services.

Just a few weeks later, on January 23, ICE expanded the focus of these raids to include teenagers under Operation Border Guardian, which advocates said represented a “new low.”

Those advocates now fear that, as a new school year begins, a new list of students will receive orders for removal, putting them at risk of being quietly deported and never heard from again.

“From Protected Class to High-Priority Target: How the ‘System Is Rigged’ Against Unaccompanied Migrant Children” is the first of a two-part series. In the coming days, Vasquez explores the community response and the troubling effects the arrests of asylum-seeking students in North Carolina have had on teachers and classmates.
