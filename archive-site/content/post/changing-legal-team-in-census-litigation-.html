Following the Trump administration's announcement to the district court that they will continue to pursue a citizenship question (updated 7/5/2019), NPR and the NY Times are reporting a shakeup in the legal team. This news is consequential for two reasons. First, the need to reach beyond the DOJ federal programs branch that is typically tasked with defending administration positions suggests that lawyers on the case felt uncomfortable arguing positions inconsistent with the ones they had taken earlier in the litigation and with the statements they made to the court. Second, the attorneys being considered for replacement are as problematic as they are telling: among them, the Attorney General is reportedly considering attorneys from the DOJ Office of Immigration Litigation among other divisions. News of the legal team is still developing, but Buzzfeed and Law.com report that court statements indicate the new legal team will include the head of the Consumer Protection Branch, a political appointee who previously served in the White House counsel's office, and the assistant director and deputy director of the Office of Immigration Litigation.

On the obligation of DOJ attorneys, who have not publicly stated a reason for withdrawal, the would be acting within bounds to refuse to represent positions contrary to the law or ethical codes. Their position within the administrative positions them as civil servants with two masters (an issue invoked by longstanding debates over the unitary executive and political control of agencies in administrative law): the Attorney General and President may be their boss, but so is Congress as the author of statutes that authorize the decennial census. Breaches of their legal mandate to follow Congress' edicts take precedent over pressure from their political boss. As a more general matter of legal ethics, attorneys have an obligation of candor to the court and fidelity to the law. Their goal is not only to zealously represent their client, the agency (though some argue the client in government cases is the "public interest" and the model code of professional responsibility espouses the goal of government as seeking "justice"). It is to zealously represent within the bounds of the law as an "officer of the court." How to resolve these tensions for government lawyers is especially tricky, but suffice it to say that the DOJ attorneys who are discontinuing their participation in what AG Barr has called "the next phase" of this saga is understandable.

The attempt to recruit OIL attorneys to defend this position is problematic for additional reasons. The immigration functions since 2002 have been divided between DHS and DOJ. The separation is deliberate: the notion is to build a firewall between immigration actions (such as ICE) and adjudication of claims (in DOJ). OIL receives less attention than EOIR's immigration courts and Board of Immigration Appeals. Its mission statement is:

The Office of Immigration Litigation oversees all civil immigration litigation, both affirmative and defensive, and it is responsible for coordinating national immigration matters before the federal district courts and circuit courts of appeals. It provides support and counsel to all federal agencies involved in alien admission, regulation, and removal under U.S. immigration and nationality statutes. Office of Immigration Litigation attorneys work closely with United States Attorneys' Offices on immigration cases. The Office of Immigration Litigation is divided into two functional sections, an Appellate Section and a District Court Section.

Recruiting OIL into the census litigation to defend the inclusion of a citizenship question - particularly when one of the recently proffered justifications from acting USCIS director is to obtain information that would ease the burden of illegal immigration - would raise worries about DOJ having access to sensitive information about citizenship status in its immigration-related functions. It also raises the specter of DHS and DOJ improperly pooling their powers (Daphna Renan) and cooperating across agencies on immigration litigation (Bijal Shah) in ways that breach Congress' intended separation: both to protect the DOJ from political influence and to protect DHS an independent forum for the review of their cases. The risk is present by virtual of the structural arrangement and the percolating rationales for including a citizenship question on the census, even if it remains to be seen whether the government will misuse the information collected.

UPDATE 7/9/2019 5pm: Judge denies Trump administration's bid for new legal team in NY case; UPDATE 7/10/2019 5pm: Judge denies Trump Adminstration's attempt to change legal team in Maryland, too.
