Australia's dual citizenship crisis continues to raise questions about what it means to be a citizen.

You'll recall that the crisis began over the summer when it was determined that several Australian politicians were dual nationals. The problem they faces was with Section 44 of the Australian constitution, which disqualifies from federal public office anyone who is "a subject or a citizen or entitled to the rights or privileges of a subject or a citizen of a foreign power."

In October, Australia's High Court unanimously decided that several federal politicians were ineligible for office because they held dual citizenship.

In the wake of that decision, Australia's prime minister  announced new rules to address this issue. 

politicians will be obliged to make a formal declaration about their citizenship status, as well as provide details about the time and place of their birth, and the time and place of birth of their parents.

If the politicians had citizenship of another country they will also be required to detail when and how they renounced it.

Current politicians will have 21 days to make the declaration, while future members of parliament will be required to make the declaration when they are elected.

That brings us to yesterday. MP Susan Lamb gave a tearful speech to parliament explaining just why she cannot provide the necessary information to verify her citizenship. The UK Home Office needs her parent's marriage certificate, but her father has passed and her mother abandoned her at the age of six.

In case you're wondering why on earth federal officials in Australia are being hounded to renounce dual nationalities that they may not have even know that they held, the answer, of course, is politics. As the BBC succinctly puts it: these citizenship challenges are arising "amid tight numbers in the House of Representatives."
