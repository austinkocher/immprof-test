In between the grief and the outrage associated with a year of anti-Asian sentiment that now includes the killing of 6 Asian American women, I've felt frustrated by the hasty assumption that the killings were not hate crimes. Giving the killer the benefit of the doubt when he claimed not to be motivated by hate - because he had a sexual addiction and the women who worked at the massage parlors were a temptation he wanted to eliminate - is a problem. Minimizing the harms of a mass killing by saying the killer had a "bad day" is a problem.  In a visceral sense, they are problems because they wreak of injustice and callous disregard for the loss of lives.

In a legal sense, they are problems, too. They skim over the element of animus by assuming away the requisite mens rea of hate and by prejudging the propriety of sentencing enhancements. In order to charge an individual with a hate crime, law enforcement must be able to prove that an offense was specifically motivated by hate. Seeing the charge through to a conviction is difficult because hate is not always reported and proving someone's mindset is tricky. But whether it is impossible depends on a close reading of the elements in state and federal law, discovery of evidence, and an adjudicatory process. 

This hasty assumption also a conceptual problem because it defies the well worn lessons of Critical Race Theory (CRT). The early teachings of CRT show that subjugation shows itself among women of color differently than in groups of women or people of color alone. The harms multiply. That is, more than adding up, the overlap of race and gender - what Kimberle Crenshaw termed intersectionality - transforms the nature of subjugation and eludes the usual frameworks of antidiscrimination.

Another problem is that a growing mass of studies show that implicit bias is common and infects the justice system: it makes crimes against disfavored groups more likely, strong legal defense less likely, and jurors and judges less fair.  In short, the law has a harder time remedying bias if it is implicit.

When CRT insights are applied to Asian American women, a new picture of what happens emerges. Racialized mysogyny in the fetishization of Asian women as sexual objects pervades media and film. It draws on stereotypes rooted in the Paige Act that barred Asian women as prostitutes in the years before the Chinese Exclusion Act and extended in war brides and other legislation associated with the Korean and Vietnamese wars. Asian American activists from the Asian American Feminist Collective and National Asian American Advancing Justice, among many others, have been trying to explain this. So have Asian American scholars and feminists such as Jennifer Ho, Erika Lee, and Leti Volpp.

Fairness requires that the legal process plays out before the accused is found guilty of any crime, let alone hate crime. But it also requires that the media and law enforcement not be premature in judging the outcome or shaping public perceptions that the Atlanta killings were not hate crimes because the suspect claimed he was motivated by sex, not race.
