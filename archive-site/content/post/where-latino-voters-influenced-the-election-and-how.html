While waiting for the presidential election results, my friend's twitter feed noted how unlikely it would be that politicians thanked African Americans in Detroit for flipping Michigan blue or Latinos in Arizona for shoring up Democratic support in Arizona. And yet, based on data from Latino Decisions and the UCLA Latino Politics and Policy Initiatives, it was readily apparent that Latino voters did have a huge effect on the election.

Maricopa County, Arizona is home to Phoenix, SB 1070 and Sheriff Joe Arpaio who became infamous for his severe immigration enforcement strategies and was eventually found in contempt of court for  committing civil rights abuses through his office's discriminatory practices. These experiences seem to have galvanized Latinos in the election. Community organizers sought to naturalize, register, and get out the vote at a level that, some say, delivered 75% of the Latino vote to Vice President Joe Biden. As explained in Roll Call:

A key rallying point for Arizona Latino voters came after the state implemented SB 1070, a restrictive anti-immigration law that allowed police to crack down on anyone believed to be an undocumented immigrant. The 2010 law sparked immediate backlash from the state’s Latino community and led grassroots organizations to politically mobilize voters. “If anyone had said that Arizona is going to turn blue 10 years ago, most people would think you’re crazy,” said Nuño-Perez of Latino Decisions.

Similar things happened in California after Proposition 187 in the 1990s. A state initiative to end public benefits for undocumented immigrants became a galvanizing force in the politicization of Latino voters there, resulting in what sociologists and others (including ImmigrationProf blogger KJ in a UC Davis Law Review symposium on Prop 187 and Paul Ong writing about Chinese Americans) have called "defensive naturalization" and unleashing significant electoral power in the populous state that many see as the face of the future for a demographically diversifying America. (My writing about defensive citizenship builds on this idea in Pursuing Citizenship in the Enforcement Era.)

In contrast, Florida's support for Donald Trump was buoyed by Latino support as well. The bulk of these Latino voters were Cuban and have traditionally voted Republican. A Roll Call article details further complexities in the political dynamics in Florida, noting that the Latino community  is composed of Cubans and also Colombians, Puerto Ricans and Venezuelans. Still, it is the Cuban Americans that get the attention of Republican presidential candidates and credit for supporting Trump:

Cuban Americans in South Florida are being credited for helping to secure the Latino support for Trump in the state. They generally lean more conservative than other Latino groups, and the vast majority have supported how the president has handled immigration, health care and the COVID-19 pandemic.

Past coverage of Latino voting dynamics describes other cross-currents within the demographic group (e.g. male vs. female support for Trump), underscoring that ethnic groups are not monolithic, that their influence varies in different parts of the country, and that the character and concerns of a community change in response to their political environment.
