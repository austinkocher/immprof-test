Travel Bans: Mormons and Muslims by Nathan B. Oman, William & Mary Law School Research Paper No. 09-472

Abstract

The essay is based on a lecture delivered as part of The Seymour David Steinman Memorial Lecture Series at McGill University on November 14, 2019. During the run up to the 2016 election, then-candidate Donald Trump issued a statement calling for “a total and complete shutdown of Muslims entering the United States until our country's Representatives can figure out what is going on.” On January 27, 2017, one week after his inauguration, now-President Donald Trump signed an executive order entitled “Protecting the Nation from Foreign Terrorist Entry into the United States.” President Trump's actions raise the issue of religious discrimination and immigration and reproduce an all-but-forgotten episode in American history: The effort of the federal government in the late 19th century to ban Mormon immigration to the United States. Recalling those events in light of President Trump’s hostility toward Muslim immigration offers some useful lessons on the intersection of religion, f ear of criminal action, and immigration. The efforts of the federal government to restrict Mormon immigration contain some useful lessons. First, there is a long history in the United States of using religion as a proxy for future criminality. Second, there are consequences when the government chooses to target people based on religion. Finally, while executive discretion has always been a feature of American immigration law, there is no reason to suppose that executive action is less likely to be infused with religious bias than legislative action. To guard against such religious bias, a minimal level of judicial review may be necessary. If, as the Court held in Hawaii v. Trump, such judicial review is not available, then political skepticism of and hostility toward religious bigotry in the context of immigration is particularly important.
