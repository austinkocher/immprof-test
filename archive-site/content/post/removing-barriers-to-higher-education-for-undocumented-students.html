--- title: "Removing Barriers to Higher Education for Undocumented Students" date: 2014-12-05T05:57:52 url: "/post/removing-barriers-to-higher-education-for-undocumented-students/" aliases: ["/immigration/2014/12/removing-barriers-to-higher-education-for-undocumented-students.html"] type: post year: "2014" category: "Current Affairs" category_slug: "current-affairs" byline: "Immigration Prof" ---

Every year, around 65,000 undocumented students graduate from American high schools, but few actually continue on to and complete a postsecondary education. However, this is not necessarily for lack of want. These students too often encounter unique legal, social, and economic barriers that prevent them from achieving their dreams.

College costs have risen dramatically throughout the country for all students, yet many undocumented students are still denied the basic education benefits available to their peers—including in-state tuition rates and financial aid. Without these benefits, many undocumented students pay around 61 percent more to attend the same college or university as their peers, even though they attended the same K-12 schools.

A new report released by Generation Progress and the Center for American Progress argues that the legal, social, and economic barriers to postsecondary education can be overcome. “Undocumented young people have led fights in the states for years to access higher education,” Zenen Jaimes Pérez,Policy Advocate for Generation Progress, said. “However, too many are still unable to access higher education. Undocumented students are a part of our community and the future of our country. They should be able to access the same higher-education benefits as their peers in order to participate in our economy.”

The report posits that a concerted effort by supportive mentors, high school counselors, and higher-education administrators can have an enormous, positive upside for undocumented students as they navigate the educational system. However, there are also steps that Congress, the Obama administration, state and local governments, and colleges and universities can take to expand postsecondary opportunities for undocumented students.

Some of the report’s recommendations include:

States should continue to pass tuition-equity legislation to allow students to qualify for in-state tuition rates and publicly funded state education benefits and to require professional development for high school and college personnel.

Colleges and universities should expand financial aid opportunities for undocumented students and admit more undocumented students.

The Obama administration should expand access to Pell Grants to undocumented students through an experimental site initiative.

Congress should pass immigration reform legislation that creates a pathway to citizenship for undocumented youth and that includes progressive education provisions.

Caught in unfair circumstances, undocumented students—most of them coming of age in the United States—face uncertain futures, their dreams and potential thwarted by roadblocks to higher education. It is up to policymakers to unblock the path to a brighter future for thousands of young, eager students and for the country as a whole.
