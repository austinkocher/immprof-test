While bold immigration proposals continue to be in the news, a lesser noticed regulatory planning document provides info on the timeline and implementation details of known policies.  An analysis of the regulatory agenda shows that the administration plans to spend the next year implementing the public charge rule thta denies green cards and visas to those who take public benefits and reforming employment visa programs to eliminate work authorization for H1-B spouses.

The agency also hints at reforms to the U.S. asylum system and procedures, dismantling protections for migrant children in government custody, beefing up its ranks of immigration judges, and increasing the U.S. Attorney General’s certification powers.

More analysis of the regulatory agenda from Law360 follows:

Visa Reform

The administration indicated it plans to publish a notice of proposed rulemaking as early as the end of May to strip employment eligibility from the foreign spouses of H-1B visa holders, amending a 2015 rule issued by former President Barack Obama’s administration as part of a series of executive actions that allowed those spouses to work under what’s called the H-4 visa program. That Obama-era rule is under review at the D.C. Circuit, where a group of American information technology workers known as Save Jobs USA is arguing that the rule violated administrative law and should be struck down. Trump’s U.S. Department of Justice has indicated that it's planning to rescind the H-4 work authorization, which would render the group’s legal challenge moot.

The administration also took aim at the H-1B visa itself, which is reserved for foreign workers in specialty occupations, mostly with bachelor’s degrees or higher, in what Pierce described as the “biggest reform to the H-1B program since it was created.” That proposal, set to be released in August, would change the criteria by which visa applicants are evaluated, including by revising the definition of a “specialty occupation.”

The administration also revealed that it will propose to do away with concurrent filings of visa petitions with I-485 applications to adjust status, a move Pierce said could be a “huge hardship” for immigrants trying to apply for immigration benefits. For example, the spouse of a U.S. citizen applying to adjust from an employment visa to a green card could not submit the green card petition and adjustment application simultaneously. Instead, that person would have to wait until U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services processes the green card petition before filing the adjustment application, potentially preventing that person from leaving the country or working in the meantime, Pierce explained.

In June, the U.S. Department of Justice is also set to publish an early version of its “public charge” regulation, which would allow the government to weigh a visa applicant’s likelihood of needing more types of public assistance when adjudicating the application. DHS, whose initial proposal was released last year, is set to publish a final rule in September. A recent Migration Policy Institute study finds that the draft regulation would disproportionately target women, children and the elderly.

Two proposals to revise the EB-5 visa program, which gives U.S. permanent residency to eligible foreign citizens who invest in the U.S. enterprises that create American jobs, are also on the table. One proposal would raise the minimum investment amount from $1 million in high employment areas and $500,000 in more rural areas to $1.8 million in high employment areas and $1.35 million in the targeted employment areas. A final version of that proposal is set to be published this month. A second proposal would encourage EB-5 investors to invest in rural areas and to increase oversight of the program.

Immigration Courts

The agenda paves the way for a series of changes this summer to the immigration court system, which is housed within DOJ’s Executive Office for Immigration Review, as the Trump administration mulls regulations that hand more authority to the U.S. Attorney General and the immigration courts’ appellate body, the Board of Immigration Appeals, and beef up the immigration courts themselves.

The DOJ plans to suggest a regulation in July allowing the attorney general to refer more types of immigration cases to himself to decide, giving him a heavier hand to shape immigration case law. Currently, the attorney general can only refer final BIA cases to himself, but this rule would let him refer immigration court decisions that have not been appealed as well as cases that are still pending before the appellate board. Pierce estimated that this draft regulation would thousands of immigration cases available to the attorney general. Former Attorney General Jeff Sessions, who stepped down in November, wielded this referral power often, notably narrowing the circumstances in which domestic abuse survivors can qualify for asylum.

The administration is also advancing proposals to change the way immigration courts are run in an attempt to eliminate the nearly 870,000-case backlog, reviving proposals from the Bush and Obama administrations to appoint more temporary immigration judges as well as one to allow the BIA to decide cases in panels of three and to affirm immigration judge decisions without explaining why in an opinion. Another proposal would increase the use of video teleconferencing in immigration courts. By resurfacing these so-called “zombie” proposals, the Trump administration does not need to put the regulations through another notice-and-comment period, allowing it to jump to the final rule stage.

Asylum and the Border

Immigration advocates can expect to see a finalized version of the Trump administration’s proposed changes to the Flores agreement — a 1997 settlement that established standards of care for migrant kids in custody — in September, as part of the administration’s bid to loosen the requirements in order to detain families together for longer periods of time. The final rule would vacate Flores, which held that minors cannot be held in detention for longer than 20 days, forcing immigration authorities to either separate them from their parents or to release the families together on bond for the duration of their immigration court proceedings.

Further attempts by the administration to reform the U.S. asylum system could also be on the horizon, with two proposals — one by DHS and the other by DOJ — that could implement “bars to asylum eligibility.” The two proposals are not scheduled to see the light until November 2019 and April 2020. Pierce said that this could be an attempt by the government to put forth similar ideas to those in the so-called asylum ban. The policy, which has been blocked by a federal court, would have stripped asylum eligibility from anyone who crossed the border in between designated ports.
