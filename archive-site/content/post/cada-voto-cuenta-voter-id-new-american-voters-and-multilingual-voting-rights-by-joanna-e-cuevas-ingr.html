Immigration Prof Blog is proud to welcome back our guest blogger, Joanna E. Cuevas Ingram, Associate Counsel at LatinoJustice PRLDEF, who writes to address the potential discriminatory impacts of voter ID laws, faced by new American and limited-English proficient (LEP) Spanish-speaking voters across the country this year:

Cada Voto Cuenta: Voter ID, New American Voters and Multilingual Voting Rights

In November 2016, the United States will experience the impact of its first Presidential Election since the Federal Voting Rights Act of 1965 (VRA) was passed, in which people will be seeking to vote without the key preclearance protections of Sections 4 and 5 the VRA, which were rendered null by the Supreme Court’s June 2013 Shelby County v. Holder decision. 

At the same time, Pew Research Center observes that "the U.S. electorate this year will be the country’s most racially and ethnically diverse ever. Nearly one-in-three eligible voters on Election Day (31%) will be Hispanic, black, Asian or another racial or ethnic minority, up from 29% in 2012. Much of this change is due to strong growth among Hispanic eligible voters, in particular U.S.-born youth."

In the absence of these key preventative anti-discrimination provisions, however, (which required certain jurisdictions with a history of voting discrimination and low turnout among historically disenfranchised voters to seek permission from the U.S. Dept. of Justice (USDOJ) or a three-judge panel in federal court in Washington D.C., before making any potentially discriminatory election changes prior to any election) and in the presence of over thirty-three (33) states implementing newly minted restrictive voter ID laws, nine (9) of which may require strict forms of photo ID--several for the first time ever in a national presidential election--many civil rights education, impact litigation and advocacy organizations are concerned about the risks of both old and new potential forms of discrimination that diverse voters of color, new American voters, LEP voters, and LEP Spanish-speaking voters may face at the polls this November.

The Impact of Shelby on Voting Rights in 2016
In primary after primary this year, civil rights advocates have seen jurisdictions once covered by the VRA making it increasingly difficult for people to exercise their right to vote. In Brooklyn, entire buildings and blocks were de-registered from voting in a purge of over 125,000 voters from the rolls.  Arizona and other states like Florida with high numbers of African American and Latina/x/o voters closed high percentages of their polling places and/or curtailed early voting.  In North Carolina, thousands of voters were turned away on Election Day because of the impact of the state’s voter suppression law.

Texas is proceeding to utilize its draconian voter ID law, SB 14, despite two federal court decisions holding that the law is racially discriminatory.  LatinoJustice PRLDEF (LJP) filed an Amicus Brief earlier this Spring in the Fifth Circuit, supporting the affirmance of the lower federal court's finding that the law is discriminatory, and would have a discriminatory impact on voters of color, including Latina/x/o voters.  LJP filed the brief together with the international law firm Milbank, Tweed, Hadley & McCloy LLP, on behalf of LJP, NALEO Educational Fund, VotoLatino, HNBA, Mi Familia Vota and other national Latina/x/o civil rights organizations.
The Joint LJP Amici Brief can be found here.
Had the VRA remained intact, these jurisdictions would have had to prove these changes didn’t deny voting rights to people of color before they took effect.  Now, without DOJ preclearance oversight and accountability, these states and localities are now enacting election changes which may have a potentially devastating discriminatory impact on Latina/x/o voters.

New American Voters, Citizenship Verification and Voter ID
 
Even more states beyond those previously covered under Sections 4 and 5 of the VRA may be engaging in elections activities that carry a discriminatory effect on voters of color, including African American, Asian American, Native American and Latina/x/o voters, as well as naturalized citizens or new American voters, who are now facing draconian citizenship verification requirements, on top of the standard oath and affirmation every American must take when they register to vote and cast a ballot.   On behalf of thousands of voters, ACLU recently won hard-fought victories in the 10th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals, in a ruling that "temporarily upholds a court order that required Kansas to allow those individuals to vote in federal elections even though they didn't provide citizenship documentation when applying or renewing their driver's licenses, as required under Kansas law. The state has said as many as 50,000 people could be affected."  ACLU also recently filed a motion in federal court in Wisconsin to request that "Wisconsin voters having trouble obtaining the required photo identification be able to cast ballots" as well -- underscoring the many contexts in which eligible voters are being forced to prove and reprove their status as American Citizens, over and over again.
 
States like Oregon and California are taking a different, more inclusive approach to voter registration, working towards full automatic voter registration, and AP reports that Oregon has already automatically registered an estimated 68,500 voters under its automatic opt-out DMV-based voter-registration system.
 
However, many states are simply not doing enough to include voters of color, new American voters and LEP voters, and local jurisdictions, even in California, found themselves in the hot seat recently during the California Primary in Siskiyou County, where Hmong residents “were terrified of going to vote” according to Lori Shellenberger, Voting Rights Director of ACLU California, in a phone interview with Talking Points Memo (TPM), after the Redding Record Searchlight reported that the Siskiyou County Sheriff's Office set up a checkpoint outside a subdivision near Hornbrook and stopped cars being driven by Hmong drivers only to ask them "whether they were registered to vote."

 TPM reports that Shellenberger noted that “There was really widespread fear,” and that “It was creating an increasing atmosphere of intimidation in that community.” TPM also observed that Shellenberger contacted the California Secretary of State’s and Attorney General’s Offices after it “became increasingly clear to me that this was potentially a targeted campaign against an ethnic minority.”

The Washington Post also recently published a video reviewing "The two sides to the voter ID law debate", yet it failed to mention that there might actually be more than just two sides.

LEP voters, in particular, in addition to having to contend with potential intimidation and misinformation, may also now have to contend with translating voter ID requirements in a number of other states.  Over 25 million residents in the U.S. are limited-English proficient (LEP).  Beyond those states and covered jurisdictions that are still required to provide language translation under Sections 4(e) and 203 of the VRA in 2016, all states should consider translating their election information, registration and ballot materials--- local and nationally vetted translation procedures and practices for voter information and balloting can directly improve voter participation among LEP voters. 
 
While many U.S. Latina/x/o voters are primarily English-speaking and native born, and the Pew Research Center recently observed that 40% of Latina/x/os born in the U.S. don't speak Spanish in the home, many Latina/x/o Americans also speak Spanish.  The Pew Research Center analysis shows that 36.7 million or nearly seventy-three percent (73%) of the total U.S. Latina/x/o population speaks Spanish at home, making Spanish the most spoken non-English language in the U.S.  According to Mark Hugo Lopez at the Pew Research Center, about a quarter of all Latina/x/o adults are English-dominant, a third are Spanish-dominant or LEP, and over 40 percent are bilingual. 

LatinoJustice PRLDEF's 2016 Cada Voto Cuenta Voter Protection Initiative, affiliated with the larger nonpartisan National Election Protection Coalition, is teaming up with the Brennan Center for Justice at NYU School of Law and Rock The Vote to translate voter registration deadlines and voter ID requirements in all 50 states into Spanish for all LEP Spanish-speaking voters to access, by the end of this Summer.

LatinoJustice noted in an interview in USA TODAY: " 'There are so many voter ID laws they can be confusing because in every state they are different,' said Joanna Cuevas Ingram, associate counsel with LatinoJustice PRLDEF...'There's a need for clarity.
We believe every vote counts and every voter should have access to information regardless of the language they speak.’  In a recent survey, LatinoJustice PRLDEF found 18 states, including Louisiana, that fail to make online voting information available in Spanish.  Yes, you read that correctly --18 states are failing to provide voting information in Spanish online in 2016.

"Cuevas Ingram said states should expand efforts to translate election material. 'It’s needed in as many languages as possible,' she said. 'Even if you’re not required to, if the goal is to ensure that everybody has access to one of our most fundamental rights, why not do it?' "
