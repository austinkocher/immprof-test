Jack Jenkins reports that a pastor and immigrant rights activist has prevailed in a lawsuit against the U.S. Department of Homeland Security. A judge ruled last week that federal officers violated her religious freedom and retaliated against her after a 2019 incident along the U.S.-Mexico border.

U.S. District Judge Todd W. Robinson ruled that Reverend Kaji Douša had established that the U.S. Customs and Border Protection had unlawfully retaliated against her for protected First Amendment activity, violated her Free Exercise right to minister to migrants, and violated the Religious Freedom Restoration Act.

Douša, the senior pastor of Park Avenue Christian Church, celebrated the ruling in a statement to Religion News Service on Monday. “I am overwhelmed with gratitude for Judge Robinson’s sound, fair and just ruling,” Douša said. “The government’s approach — stalling, gaslighting, even lying — was entirely unconvincing to the court, and I am thrilled for the vindication. Judge Robinson cleared my name, and I thank God for it.”

The case dates back to January 2019, under President Donald Trump’s administration, when Douša was stopped and questioned for roughly an hour-and-a-half by federal officers while returning from Tijuana, Mexico, to the United States. Douša had been participating in a 40-day “Sanctuary Caravan” assisting Central American asylum seekers in Mexico.

A media outlet later published documents from a U.S. government database of people involved with the caravan that included Douša’s name and photo with a yellow “X” across her face. The documents suggested the pastor’s pass allowing for expedited screening at the border had been revoked.

Douša sued the government. Documents produced in the lawsuit revealed evidence that the U.S. government surveilled and investigated Douša for her activism, at one point tying her to antifa. “To be honest I don’t even know what antifa is,” Douša said in 2020. The government’s actions appeared to focus on marriages performed for asylum seekers by Douša.

Douša';s lawyers accused the government of targeting her “because she engaged in ‘acts of devotion commanded by the core tenets of her Christian faith’ — namely, advocating for and ministering to migrants in New York and at the Southern Border by blessing their marriages, dedicating their children to Christ, hearing their confessions, and providing them other forms of pastoral care.” Such actions, Douša lawyers argued, are protected under the Constitution and the Religious Freedom Restoration Act.
