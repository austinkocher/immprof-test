INA § 212(a)(2), 8 U.S.C. § 1182(a)(2), reads: "any alien convicted of, or who admits having committed, or who admits committing acts which constitute the essential elements of (I) a crime involving moral turpitude ... or (II) a violation of ... any law ... relating to a controlled substance" is inadmissible.

In the flurry of published BIA opinions, you might have missed Matter of Mendez Maldonado, 30 I&N Dec. 62 (BIA 2026), which concerns the above highlighted text.

In his USCIS interview for adjustment of status, Mendez Maldonado "admitted in his sworn testimony that he possessed and used cocaine in Arizona."

Mendez Maldonado said this wasn't an "admission" of a controlled substance offense under Matter of K-, 7 I&N Dec. 594, 597 (BIA 1957) ("“a valid admission of a crime for immigration purposes requires that the alien be given an adequate definition of the crime, including all essential elements, and that it be explained in understandable terms.”)

The BIA held that Matter of K- and its predecessor Matter of J-, 2 I&N Dec. 285, 287–88 (BIA 1945) (listing six requirements for obtaining an admission), "exceeded any statutory or regulatory requirements."

Now, factually, the USCIS officer who interviewed Mendez Maldonado followed the guidance of Matter of K-, walking through the relevant state law and the illegal nature of cocaine use. Still, the BIA took the opportunity to reject those earlier cases, writing: "All that is required for an alien to be inadmissible under section 212(a)(2)(A)(i)(II) of the INA, 8 U.S.C. § 1182(a)(2)(A)(i)(II), is that the alien admits to committing acts which constitute the essential elements of a controlled substance offense." And, in this case, Mendez Maldonado "replied 'correct' when the USCIS officer asked him if he knew what constituted a cocaine violation in Arizona... [and] replied 'that is correct' when the USCIS officer asked if he knew that using cocaine was illegal in Arizona."
