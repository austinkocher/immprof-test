From Honor to Contempt: The Diplomatic Baseline and the Proof of Animus After Mullin v. Doe by Hashim Mohamed

Abstract

In 1962, a President of the United States welcomed Somalia’s Prime Minister with full military honors and praised a separation of powers that resembled his own country’s. In 2025 and 2026, the same office said that Somalis “contribute nothing,” called Somalia “not a nation,” and ended the immigration protection Somali nationals had held since 1991. Mullin v. Doe (2026) then left constitutional claims standing and treated a race-neutral explanation for official statements as enough to defeat them. Weeks later, a federal court found that Somali plaintiffs had shown irreparable harm but not a likelihood of success. This Article asks what evidence can break that impasse, and it finds the answer in the government’s own archive.

Three literatures circle this problem without solving it. Scholars of animus, among them Reva Siegel, Dale Carpenter, and Elise Boddie, have shown how the purpose requirement hardened toward malice and how courts let the passage of time discount history, yet they offer no benchmark against which to measure a hostile word. Katherine Shaw and other scholars of presidential speech have asked when courts may hear what a President says, but not what they should compare it with. Louis Henkin, Hiroshi Motomura, and Gabriel Chin have exposed the racial roots of immigration exceptionalism, and Adam Cox has retraced its origins, yet no one has turned that work into a rule of evidence. This Article proposes the missing benchmark, a diplomatic baseline. Where the government’s own archives preserve an internal, official, specific, and documentary characterization of a national group, that record lets a court exclude the benign explanation Mullin accepts, because a general stance on immigration cannot explain a reversal confined to one nationality. Nor does the hearsay rule stand in the way, because the government’s own words, offered against it, are statements of an opposing party.

Critics will argue that evidence from the 1960s is too remote, that Somalia’s collapse after 1991 changed everything, that state ceremony is mere flattery, and that Trump v. Hawaii demands deference to any plausible neutral reason. Drawing on the government’s own records, the Article answers each, showing that what changed was the Somali state and Washington’s uses for it, not the capacities of the Somali people, and that the government’s present conduct contradicts the premises of its own notice. The method has limits, which the Article marks, and it is viewpoint neutral. Any litigant, of any national origin, may hold any administration to its own word.
