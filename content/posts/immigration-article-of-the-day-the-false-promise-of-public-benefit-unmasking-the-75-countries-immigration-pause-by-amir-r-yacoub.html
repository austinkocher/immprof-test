The False Promise of Public Benefit: Unmasking the 75 Countries Immigration Pause by Amir R. Yacoub

Abstract

On January 14, 2026, the Department of State announced a sweeping, indefinite pause on all immigrant visa issuances for nationals of 75 countries, effective January 21, 2026, premised upon a blanket presumption that applicants from these nations represent a high risk of becoming public charges. This Article argues that the pause is unlawful and should be set aside under the APA standard on multiple independent grounds. First, the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) affirmatively mandates individualized visa adjudication and prohibits categorical nationality-based bans of the kind the Department has imposed. Second, the consular nonreviewability doctrine — routinely invoked by the government as a shield — is structurally inapplicable here because the policy suppresses rather than reflects individual consular discretion. Third, robust public charge screening tools already exist and require case-by-case assessment. Fourth, the blanket pause cannot logically extend to self-sufficient visa categories such as EB-5, EB-2 NIW, and EB-1A. Fifth, the Department has failed to satisfy even the minimal reasoned explanation demanded by the Administrative Procedure Act (APA), a bar that is now higher following the Supreme Court's elimination of Chevron deference in Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo. Sixth, the pause is functionally final agency action. Seventh, if the executive branch wishes to reduce immigration from certain countries, the constitutional avenue runs through Congress, not the State Department. Eighth, this policy is not a Presidential Proclamation under INA § 212(f) and thus cannot claim the deference courts have extended to executive proclamations. This Article then proceeds to systematically refute the government's sur-reply in Storie v. Trump, No. 1:26-cv-00567 (D.D.C.), demonstrating that the government's 'containment strategy' fails on the merits, on the law, and in the face of documented human harm. Ninth, even if Trump v. CASA, Inc. (2025) restricts a district court's authority to issue universal preliminary injunctions under the general equitable power of the Judiciary Act of 1789, it does not foreclose the APA's own mandatory remedial provision. Under 5 U.S.C. § 706(2), a reviewing court shall 'hold unlawful and set aside' unlawful agency action — a statutory remedy that operates as vacatur with erga omnes effect, extending to all persons, not merely the named plaintiffs. The CASA majority expressly declined to resolve 'the distinct question whether the [APA] authorizes federal courts to vacate federal agency action,' and Justice Kavanaugh's concurrence confirmed that district courts may 'preliminarily set aside or decline to set aside an agency rule under the APA.' Because the Department of State is unambiguously an 'agency' within the meaning of the APA — unlike the President, who is not — the § 706(2) set-aside mechanism applies in full and is available to District Courts.
