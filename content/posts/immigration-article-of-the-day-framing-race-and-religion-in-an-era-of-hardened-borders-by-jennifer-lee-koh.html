Framing Race and Religion in an Era of Hardened Borders by Jennifer Lee Koh, 25 Rutgers Journal of Law and Religion 230 (2026)

Abstract

2026 reflects a critical juncture when it comes to the intersections of race, religion, law, and migration in the European Union and United States. Borders have hardened across both continents due to political sentiment, and reinforced by the power of law. The U.S. government pursues an unrelenting mass deportation agenda that has acutely harmed communities of color, and both the U.S. and various E.U. states have responded to the challenge of global migration by ending access to asylum. In some quarters, religious freedom is flourishing, especially for conservatively-oriented Christians. Both race and religion have served as markers of national identity, so that certain religious communities experience the brunt of racial discrimination on top of skepticism around their religious freedom claims. From the perspective of those advocating for the humanity and rights of migrants on the ground, the terrain is filled with urgency and regression.

This Comment responds to a symposium involving scholars of migration, race and religion. I draw attention to two central issues that emerge from the symposium, and do so from the perspective of a legal scholar whose primary focus is U.S. immigration law. The first issue involves race. How should law and discourse approach race and racism, and how might frameworks governing race affect practical interventions around migration? The second issue centers on religion. What does religion do to enhance our understanding of how race and racism operate against the backdrop of migration? The symposium addresses important dimensions of these questions, and the rise of authoritarianism in the West means that they will continue to generate a host of additional issues for future exploration.
