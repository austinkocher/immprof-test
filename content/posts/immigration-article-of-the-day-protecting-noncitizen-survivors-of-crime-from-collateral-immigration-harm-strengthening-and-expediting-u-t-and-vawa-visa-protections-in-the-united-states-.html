Protecting Noncitizen Survivors of Crime from Collateral Immigration Harm: Strengthening and Expediting U-, T-, and VAWA Visa Protections in the United States by Rajeev MeenaProtecting Noncitizen Survivors of Crime from Collateral Immigration Harm: Strengthening and Expediting U-, T-, and VAWA Visa Protections in the United States by Rajeev Meena

Abstract

Another victimization that immigrants who have survived crime in the United States are usually involved in is the second victimization by their abusers and, secondly, victimization by an immigration system that puts them under detention and deportation in the wrong belief that they seek justice. This essay reviews the organizational and operational inefficiencies of the U, T, and Violence Against Women Act (VAWA) visa laws alongside how implementation-level processes, including the use of detainers, 287(g) arrangements, and data-sharing, continue to cause collateral immigration injury. Based on the statutory analysis, federal directions, empirical research, and the models of other countries, including Canada, the United Kingdom, and the European Union, the study reveals the key gaps between the legislative intent and actions. It suggests specific reforms such as the statutory periods of reflection, codified areas of protection, and the compulsory confidentiality measures that will help make sure that the survivors can report the crime without being afraid. Amplifying and hastening these humanitarian safeguards is not only vital to the fairness of immigrants but also to the wholeness and effectiveness of the U.S. legal structure, per se.
